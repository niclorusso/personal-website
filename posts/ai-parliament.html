What the Swiss parliament asks about AI

272 motions, postulates, interpellations and questions about artificial intelligence, from 2000 to today: what they ask, who files them and what the Federal Council answers.

I collected every item filed in the Swiss parliament from 2000 to 3 November 2026 that mentions artificial intelligence: motions, postulates, interpellations and questions. In 272 of them AI is the main subject or a substantial part. An AI model read each text and noted its subject and what it asks for. The interactive page has the charts and the full list.

The main findings:

  • Between 1 January and 3 November 2026 the items about AI are 79, against 42 in the same period of 2025.
  • 67% of the items filed by members ask for rules or safeguards, 19% ask to promote AI.
  • What an item asks depends more on its subject than on the party of its author.
  • The Federal Council has answered 110 motions and postulates and proposed to reject 85 of them.

Below is the story in order, and at the end how far the numbers can be trusted.

2017: robots

Until 2016 there are a handful of items. The first cluster comes in March 2017, with seven items in one session. Mathias Reynard asks to examine a tax on robots and a legal personality for robots. Lisa Mazzone asks for a report on the same tax and cites a proposal of the European Parliament's legal affairs committee. Beat Flach asks for an international ban on autonomous weapons. The Federal Council proposed to reject all four. The robot tax does not come back under that name.

2021 and 2022: faces and algorithms

In 2021 the count rises to 17 items, and the subject changes to surveillance. The Greens file 11 of the 27 items of these two years. Balthasar Glättli asks whether facial recognition in public space should be regulated or banned, two weeks after the European Commission presented the AI Act. In 2022 an interpellation follows press reports that Swiss police forces had tested Clearview. Several items ask for a public register of the algorithms the administration uses.

2023: ChatGPT

The first item that names ChatGPT is a written question by Jörg Mäder of 15 December 2022, two weeks after its release. In 2023 the items are 39, against 10 the year before, and 32 of them are filed between January and June. The requests become general: Marcel Dobler asks for a report on the legal gaps, the Social Democratic group asks to take over the EU AI Act. In this period 85% of the items ask for rules or safeguards, the highest share of any period. The Federal Council proposed to reject both. After June the wave stops: seven items in the second half of the year.

2024: one issue at a time

In 2024 there is no peak, 34 items, and they are about single problems: the use of journalistic and creative works by generative AI, the electricity that AI needs, apps that undress people in photos. The Federal Council accepted the motion by Petra Gössi on copyright.

2025: chips and sovereignty

In January 2025 the United States limited the export of AI chips and put Switzerland in the second tier. In March Simone Gianini (from my hometown) filed an urgent question about it. From then on dependence on foreign providers becomes a regular theme: 24 of the 27 items that raise it are from 2025 and 2026. It is also the period most favourable to AI: between January 2025 and April 2026 a third of the items ask to promote it, against 7% in 2023.

The other trigger is the Federal Council itself. On 12 February 2025 it decided to ratify the Council of Europe convention on AI and to regulate sector by sector. Min Li Marti asked why the plan was so limited.

2026: twice as many

Between 1 January and 3 November 2026 the items about AI are 79, against 42 in the same period of 2025. 52 different members filed one, against 34 in the whole of 2025. The Centre files as many as the Social Democrats for the first time, 24 and 23.

The items name what set them off. In March four of them name Grok and sexualised deepfakes; the Federal Council accepted the motion by Raphaël Mahaim, after proposing to reject two earlier ones on the same subject. In June Anthropic switched off its two most capable models, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5, worldwide, on an order of the US authorities; the block was later lifted. Estelle Revaz filed an interpellation about it within days, and at least four other items between June and November refer to it and ask what Switzerland would do if access were cut again.

In October members of all six groups filed the same motion: Switzerland should push for international minimum safety standards for the most powerful models at the AI summit it hosts in Geneva in 2027. Gerhard Andrey filed the first copy. It is one of nine texts filed in identical form by members of different groups, six of them in 2026.

What does not change

Across the whole period, 67% of the items filed by members ask for rules or safeguards and 19% ask to promote AI. What an item asks depends more on its subject than on the party of its author. On deepfakes, surveillance, justice and the protection of minors, more than nine items in ten ask for rules, from every group. On the economy and innovation, 62% ask to promote AI. Among the groups only the Greens stand out, with 92% of their items asking for rules.

The Federal Council has answered 110 of the motions and postulates and proposed to reject 85 of them, 77%. It rejected the general regulation asked by the left and also the reports on deregulation asked by the right. It accepted narrow changes to existing law: copyright, product liability, deepfakes in criminal law.

Two subjects are almost absent. In the whole period, 5 items are about health and 6 about education.

How far to trust the numbers

The scores come from an AI model, which read the text of each item and followed a written rubric: whether AI is its subject, what it is about and what it asks for. I ran three checks.

  • A second reading of 60 items gave the same score for nine in ten.
  • A second reading of 90 items taken at random agreed on 78, and found 5 that are not about AI and 7 that are borderline.
  • Of 240 items taken at random among the 11,508 filed from 2023 to 2026 and not selected, none was about AI.

So the scores are good enough to compare large groups of items, not to judge a single one, and a few of the 272 probably do not belong. The rubric and the method are on the interactive page.
